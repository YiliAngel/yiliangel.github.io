Overview

A body-worn haptic system that guides blind and low-vision runners through turns and hazards — without relying on audio, which competes with the ambient sound runners depend on. The goal is to let a runner feel where to go, directly on the body, and turn themselves.

Today's practice: tethered guidance from a sighted guide — safe and trusted, but it needs a human and isn't independent. PACER aims to give that guidance back to the runner.

The Device

The wearable is a lightweight spine exoskeleton — a single articulated column that runs down the back, held by shoulder straps and a waist belt — carrying a small set of distributed tactile tiles. Each tile pairs a rigid backing shell with a tensioned strap so the cue presses into the skin instead of pushing the belt away.

  • Tactile tiles at the back of the neck and along the spine, each a left/right pair. A central seesaw (rocker) presses one side into the back while the other lifts, and a lateral rack-and-pinion drags the pair sideways — together encoding direction, turn side, and urgency.
  • Head cue = gentle passive assist (the head is light and naturally leads a turn); body cue = active (the device cues, the runner turns). It guides intent rather than forcing the torso.
  • Closed-loop turn angle from an on-body IMU, so a cue can stop once the runner has turned to the target.
  • Controller, motor drivers, and battery sit in a soft rear-waist pouch; an iOS companion app carries what touch can't (pace, environment) by voice.

Research Question

The mechanism I am studying is sequential (cranio-caudal) versus simultaneous cueing — whether cueing the tiles in a head-to-pelvis sequence communicates a turn more clearly than cueing all at once. This is grounded in turning biomechanics, where healthy turning cascades head→trunk→pelvis. A pilot then tests whether runners can identify the intended turning angle from the cue alone.

A planned turn cues the tiles simultaneously; an emergency turn cues them sequentially, top→bottom — a head-to-pelvis wave grounded in cranio-caudal turning biomechanics.

Computer Vision

  • Forward sensing via a phone camera running an on-device object detector (YOLO, four classes: person, obstacle, ground-hazard, vehicle), trained on our own greenway running footage and deployed to iPhone (CoreML) for real-time, offline alerts.
  • Rear sensing via a Grove Vision AI edge module that flags people or vehicles approaching from behind — the highest-value hazard case for a runner.
  • A decision engine ranks detections by urgency and drives the haptic tiles (and voice) only when the situation changes, keeping cues sparse and legible.

Evaluation

In-field evaluation with blind and low-vision runners, to test whether the cues actually work at running speed and in real environments.

A manuscript on this work is in preparation.
